Archetype Baseline

The Baseline tab in the Deck Workspace answers a question a single decklist cannot: which of these cards is the archetype, and which ones are my choices?

It takes every cached decklist of the archetype and keeps only the cards that appear in all of them, at the count that even the leanest list can afford. Everything else — the cards some lists run and others do not, and the copies above that shared floor — is reported as the slots that are actually yours.

The tab says the same thing at the top of the page: “Every card that appears in every cached list of this archetype, at the count the smallest list can afford. Anything one list drops is left out, so the baseline is normally under 60/15 — the rest is yours.”

Which archetype it measures

The tab has no format or archetype pickers of its own, on purpose — a second pair could disagree with the ones you are already using. It measures the archetype of the deck on screen. With no deck loaded it falls back to whatever you have selected in Metagame Research. When neither gives it an archetype the header reads No archetype for the deck on screen.

There is no compute button and no cut-off to tune. The baseline recomputes itself in the background as soon as the selection changes.

The four groups

The results are a tree. The first two groups are the baseline itself; the rest is what is left over.

GroupWhat is in it
Baseline — same count in every list The staples: cards every list in the pool runs, at the same count. If every Affinity list plays four of a card, it is here at four. Nothing about this card is a choice.
Baseline — in every list, fixed at the lowest count The partial staples: cards every list runs, but not all at the same count. The baseline fixes them at the lowest count anyone plays, and each row shows the range as well — a card played as three or four reads 3–4, with the baseline fixing three. The fourth copy is a choice, so it appears among the flex candidates too.
Not this archetype The decks thrown out of the pool before anything was measured, each with how much it had in common with the rest. See The pool below. This group only appears when something was actually dropped.
Flex candidates — not in every list, ranked by play rate Everything competing for the slots the baseline leaves open: cards at least one list leaves out, and the copies of a partial staple that sit above its floor (marked above its floor). Each row gives the share of lists that play the card and its average count among those lists, so the near-misses are at the top.

Every row is labelled main or sideboard. The two zones are counted separately throughout — four copies in the sideboard is a different fact from four in the maindeck.

The line under the tree sums it up: how many decks were measured, how many slots the baseline fixes, and how many are flex. When decks were dropped it also says how many of how many were kept.

Why the baseline is smaller than 60/15

A card is in the baseline only if every single list in the pool plays it. One list leaving it out is enough to keep it out entirely, because at that point the card’s range starts at zero and there is no honest count to fix it at. There is no tolerance and no threshold — no “in 90% of lists” setting, anywhere.

That is the point rather than a limitation. Any cut-off would be somebody’s arbitrary opinion about how much disagreement still counts as consensus, and the whole value of the answer is that it contains no such opinion: every card in the baseline is one that every list committed to, at a count every list can afford. A baseline that comes to 52 maindeck cards is telling you that eight slots are genuinely contested — it is not a list that failed to reach sixty.

The pool, and the decks dropped from it

Because the baseline is a strict intersection, one deck that is not really the archetype does not make the answer slightly worse — it destroys it. A single unrelated list can cut a healthy twelve-card baseline to nothing, since there is often no card at all that both decks play.

The archetype labels that decklists arrive with are not reliable enough to trust on their own, so the pool is filtered first, by one rule: a deck with nothing in common with the others is not part of the group, whatever it is labelled.

  • Each deck is reduced to the set of card names in its maindeck. Sideboards are left out, because they swing hardest with the local metagame and two pilots of one archetype can agree on sixty cards and disagree on fifteen.
  • Two decks are compared by how much they overlap: cards in both, over cards in either.
  • A deck’s score is the average of that against every other deck in the pool. Below 30% it is dropped, and listed under Not this archetype with its score.

Counts are ignored in this comparison — four copies and one copy both just mean “runs that card”. Ratios are what the baseline itself measures; they are not what tells two archetypes apart. Nothing here reads what a card does, so there is no card list to maintain and nothing to go stale.

Filtering needs at least two decks to compare, and it finds the odd ones out — not the mislabelled ones. If half a pool were some other deck, that other deck would stop looking unusual. This is a guard against a contaminated pool, not a substitute for the labels being right.

Below that a strict intersection fails in the flattering direction: a pool of one deck is its own intersection and would report all sixty cards as settled archetype consensus. Each extra list can only remove cards, so the number is really “how many independent lists must agree before agreement means anything”. Browsing the archetype in Metagame Research caches more lists.

The baseline and a deck’s history

When you save a brand-new deck whose archetype already has a baseline, that baseline becomes the first version in the deck’s version history. Every later version then descends from it, so “how does my build differ from the archetype?” is just a diff against that first version — pin it with Set as diff baseline in the History tab.

It is written once, when the deck first gets a history, and never rewritten afterwards. A baseline drifts as the metagame moves; the one your deck was rooted at stays the snapshot it was, so what you are comparing against does not change under you.
